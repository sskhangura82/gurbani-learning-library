Read the evidence

  1. Marchant and colleagues, 2025

    In 84 students, 5:5 and 4:6 breathing generally produced larger immediate heart-rate-variability changes than box or 4–7–8 breathing. There was no meaningful mood or blood-pressure improvement, and mild overbreathing occurred. One session cannot establish lasting benefits.

    Read the original study abstract

  2. Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh, 2023

    In a four-week trial of 400 adults, slower paced breathing did not improve mental health more than a credible comparison at a faster pace. Both groups improved.

    Read the placebo-controlled trial

  3. Yilmaz Balban and colleagues, 2023

    A small exploratory trial compared three breathing methods with mindfulness. Cyclic sighing showed the clearest mood advantage; box breathing did not establish the same advantage. Box timings were individualized, so this was not simply a test of four-second counts.

    Read the breathing and mindfulness trial

  4. Fincham and colleagues, 2023

    A review found modest average stress improvements across different breathing methods. Study-quality concerns and limited follow-up prevent strong promises about any one method.

    Read the review of randomized trials

  5. McAllister and colleagues, 2026

    In 66 adults, brief supervised box breathing or periodic prolonged exhalation reduced some responses to a laboratory stress task. HRV, cortisol and cognitive performance did not differ between groups. This does not establish lasting benefits; the exhale method differed from our 4:6 rhythm.

    Read the randomized stress-task study

Heart-rate variability is variation in the time between heartbeats. A change during a breathing exercise does not by itself demonstrate better health or lasting calm.

Let comfort set the pace

Breathe gently. There is no need to fill or empty your lungs completely. Stop if you feel dizzy, short of breath or more distressed, and let your breathing return to its usual rhythm. You can leave the guide at any time.

Breath attention does not suit every moment. You can look around the room, keep your eyes open or choose another activity. Meditation does not always feel relaxing.

Different traditions, named clearly

A traditional source explains a teaching in its own setting. A clinical study asks a different kind of question. Both deserve accurate attribution.

Buddhist mindfulness of breathing

The Ānāpānasati Sutta, MN 118, includes awareness of long and short breaths within a wider contemplative training. Our natural-breath introduction is a modern starting point, not the complete traditional practice.

Read MN 118, translated by Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu

Yoga and prāṇāyāma

Yoga Sūtra 2.49 discusses breath regulation within a broader path. The linked edition includes Rama Prasada's translation and commentaries. Our modern paced patterns are not presented as exact prescriptions from this passage.

Read Yoga Sūtra 2.49, Rama Prasada edition, 1924

Stoic reflection

Epictetus distinguishes what depends on us from what does not. That distinction can inform reflection about a next action. It is philosophical guidance, not an ancient breathing prescription.

Read Handbook chapter 1, translated by Elizabeth Carter

Sikh remembrance and learning

The Simran room is a separate space within this library. This breathing guide does not prescribe a rhythm for Naam, Gurbani or Sikh practice. Fuller teaching belongs with its Sikh sources and careful study.

A growing research catalogue

Cyclic sighing, 4–7–8, alternate-nostril breathing and humming are being considered separately. Their instructions and evidence differ. They are not additional guided exercises in this preview.

A longer list alone does not make a better practice. Each addition needs an accurate source, an understandable guide and an honest account of its limits.
